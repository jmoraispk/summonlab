## Family visual grammar

- A humanoid torso rises from a long coiled serpent body; the T1–T2 forms have two arms and no hood, while hood and additional arms begin only at T3.
- Emerald, jade, deep teal, gold, bronze, ivory, and jewel-toned scale patterns remain the family palette.
- Scale, gold, gemstone inlay, silk sash, and temple stone remain separate readable materials rather than a seamless magical body.
- Arm bands, gold scale bands, jeweled brow pieces, sacred script, and later hood crests communicate rank.
- Coil direction, weapon-bearing hands, hood angle where present, and exposed scale seams preserve attack and counterplay readability.

**Escalation axis:** Coil length, scale ornament, temple mechanism authority, hood complexity from T3 onward, and hand count grow from a mobile two-armed skirmisher to a sovereign relic court.

**This form's visual delta:** Longer patterned coils, twin curved daggers, and simple gold bands mature the baseline while retaining two arms and no hood.

## Combat reading

### Battlefield duty

Hold a repeatable curved front line by bracing longer coils and threatening opposite arcs with two curved daggers.

### Teaching goal

Teach that the two armed arcs leave the coil interior safe during a committed crossed cut.

### Signature mechanic

Crossed Crescent: the two-armed, hoodless Coilguard plants patterned coils and sweeps twin daggers from the outer curve toward one crossing point.

### Telegraph

Simple gold arm bands touch, both curved blades turn inward, and the patterned outer coil flattens against the floor.

### Counterplay

Enter the coil interior before the blades cross or strike the flattened outer scale row to break the brace.

### Lower-form interaction

A Serpentling can fire across the guarded crescent, but entering the coil interior disrupts both its recoil curve and the Soldier's crossing point.
